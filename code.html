अर्जुनविषादयोग

Arjuna Vishada Yoga

(अर्जुन का विषाद और मोह)

✦

47 Shlokas • Kurukshetra Battlefield

lightbulb Central Theme

The profound crisis of moral identity, attachment, and the overwhelming confusion of duty when personal grief clashes with righteous action.

Introduction

The opening chapter sets the cosmic stage of the battlefield at Kurukshetra. King Dhritarashtra asks his royal charioteer Sanjaya to narrate the confrontation between the Kauravas and the Pandavas. Arjuna, the greatest warrior, requests Lord Krishna to drive his chariot between the two armies. Upon seeing beloved elders, teachers, cousins, and friends assembled to fight unto death, Arjuna's limbs tremble, his bow Gandiva slips from his hands, and he collapses in overwhelming grief and despair.

Main Topics Covered

1

Gathering on the Field of Dharma (Verses 1–11)

Dhritarashtra’s inquiry, Duryodhana inspecting both legions, and conches sounding.

2

Chariot Between the Armies (Verses 12–25)

Krishna places the chariot amidst both forces upon Arjuna's request to see whom he must fight.

3

Arjuna’s Despondency & Dilemma (Verses 26–47)

Grief, fear of destroying family traditions (Kuladharma), and Arjuna casting down his bow.

menu_book English Meaning

Dhritarashtra said: O Sanjaya, assembled on the sacred plain of Kurukshetra, eager to engage in battle, what did my sons and the sons of Pandu do?

explore Context — What is happening here?

King Dhritarashtra is blind both physically and spiritually. Possessive of his offspring, he refers to his sons as "Mamakah" (mine) while separating the Pandavas. The battlefield is termed Dharmakshetra—a sacred field of righteous duty—foreshadowing that righteousness shall prevail over ego.

psychology Life Lesson

Selfish possessiveness blinds our moral compass. When we divide the world strictly into "mine" versus "theirs", we invite strife and lose the ability to see higher truth and universal justice.

self_improvement Spiritual Meaning

Kurukshetra is also the human psyche. The battle represents the perennial inner conflict between the unruly impulses of the ego (the Kauravas) and the noble qualities of virtue and contemplation (the Pandavas).

help Reflection Question

In what areas of your daily life are you dividing situations into "mine" and "theirs", and where might attachment be clouding your fair judgment?
